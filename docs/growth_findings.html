The Leverage Cliff

A return-maximising leveraged book on the Nasdaq-100 compounded at 16.3%/yr out of sample against the index's 9.7%, at half the drawdown. Nearly all of that came from the timing rules — and 93% of the timing edge was earned in one 26-month window.

More leverage stops paying long before it stops hurting

Compound growth rises linearly in leverage and falls quadratically, so there is an interior optimum and a cliff just past it. On the Nasdaq-100, constant leverage financed at the bill rate plus 50 bp peaks at 2.10×. At 3× the book returns barely more than holding the index outright — and gives up 99.98% on the way. At 5× it loses money outright.

The Kelly estimate from sample moments agrees closely with the realised peak — 2.13× against 2.10×. That agreement is less reassuring than it looks. A moving-block bootstrap puts the 90% interval for the optimum at 1.18× to 3.10×, because the optimum is a ratio of two noisily estimated moments and the numerator is the equity risk premium.

Betting the point estimate is betting the whole interval. At the top of that interval you are past the cliff.

The uncomfortable part

Where the edge was actually earned

The strategy beat equally-risky constant leverage by 596 bp/yr. Decomposing that advantage day by day across the out-of-sample period gives a result that no amount of averaging should be allowed to hide.

A trend overlay is a synthetic straddle. It bleeds a small premium in choppy markets and pays enormously in one sustained collapse. The Nasdaq's 2000-02 was −82.5% and more than covered forty years of premium. The S&P's bears were never big enough to.

This is not an argument against the design — it is a description of what the design is. The payoff profile is deliberately asymmetric: against equal risk the strategy was 17 to 44 percentage points shallower in every extended bear market, and 4 to 13 points deeper in every sharp V-shaped shock. The asymmetry points the right way. But it is an average over roughly two observations.

Choosing a point

“Maximise return” has no answer until you name a drawdown

From a 2,048-point search over eight parameters, the best achievable compound return at each level of pain. Two things stand out: at a 30% tolerance the strategy already matches buy-and-hold's return with a third of its drawdown, and the unconstrained maximum stops at −58% rather than −99% — the strategy's own risk controls bind before any external limit does.

So what would you actually hold?

Leveraged Nasdaq-100, sized to a volatility target of roughly 45%, capped near 3×, gated off when the index falls below trend, with a hard drawdown throttle underneath. That ran at 1.78× average leverage and 16.3%/yr out of sample against QQQ's 9.7%.

Three things to be clear-eyed about

  1. A −50% drawdown is the design target, not the bad case. The unconstrained optimum sits at −58%. If 30% is the real limit, the honest answer is 16.4%/yr, not 23%.
  2. Most of the measured timing edge is one event. The leverage and the volatility sizing generalise. Budget for the trend overlay to cost a little and protect a lot — not to add return every year.
  3. The vehicle matters more than the parameters. Portfolio margin financed with box spreads beat TQQQ by 439 bp/yr, which is larger than most of the parameter differences in the entire search.

What would change the conclusion

  • A bear market that is fast rather than slow. Every episode where the strategy lost ground was V-shaped: 1994, 1998, 2015-16, Q4 2018, March 2020.
  • A decade with no sustained drawdown at all. The overlay's premium is paid continuously and collected rarely.
  • Financing above the modelled bill + 50 bp. At 3× assumed costs the result falls from 23.5% to 20.3% — survivable, but the margin is not unlimited.
  • The Nasdaq-100's forty-year record is partly the story of the technology sector winning. A leveraged bet on it is a leveraged bet that this continues.
