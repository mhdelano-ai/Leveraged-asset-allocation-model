Forward-looking Kelly · built from yields observable 20 August 2026

Kelly at Today's Yields

Run on 33 years of history, Kelly asked for 17× gross exposure and a backtested ladder rewarded leverage all the way to the Reg-T cap. Run on what these four asset classes actually yield today, full Kelly is 1.18× equities and nothing in bonds — and the growth-optimal leverage is roughly “fully invested”. The entire difference is the risk premium, and none of it is a modelling choice.

The inputs, and where each number comes from

Bonds: the index yield to maturity, which is what a constant-maturity bond fund earns over a holding period as the pull to par offsets price moves. Equities: the Grinold-Kroner build-up — income you can observe, plus growth and inflation you have to assume, stated separately so you can move them one at a time.

Three observations that do most of the work. Inflation is not a guess — 2.34% is the 10-year TIPS breakeven. The hedged foreign bond yield includes the hedge carry: a dollar investor rolling FX forwards earns roughly the short-rate differential, worth +1.6pp on euro bonds and +2.6pp on Japanese ones today, which is why a 3.05% Bund and a 2.65% JGB come back as 4.63% in dollars. And the arithmetic column is the one Kelly uses: it is the geometric build-up plus σ²/2, worth 1.5pp on equities — larger than the entire bond risk premium.

Volatility and correlation are measured over the last five years, not the last thirty, because the stock/bond correlation regime changed in 2022. It is currently +0.22 between US equities and US bonds, against −0.30 or lower through 2000–2020. Bonds are no longer paid to be a hedge.

Kelly leverage scales with the premium and inversely with variance. Halve the premium and you halve the leverage; there is no other moving part.

Bonds get nothing, and the reason is arithmetic

US bonds yield 4.85% and hedged foreign bonds 4.63%, against cash at 3.80% — a premium of about one point. Robinhood lends at 5.00%.

You cannot borrow at 5.00% to buy a 4.70% expected return. That single inequality removes both bond sleeves from every levered solution: at zero financing cost the optimiser wants 2.4× bonds, and at any realistic retail borrowing spread it wants none. Bonds re-enter only below 1× gross, as a substitute for cash — and at a 3.80% bill rate they are barely that either, since the extra point of yield costs six points of volatility and now moves with equities rather than against them.

This is the cleanest example on any of these pages of a conclusion that is structural rather than statistical. It does not depend on 33 years of data, on which fund proxied which asset class, or on the survivorship problem that made the historical foreign-bond weight untrustworthy. It depends on two numbers you can look up today.

The backtest ladder, re-run forward

400 bootstrapped ten-year paths through the same Reg-T account: historical daily shocks — volatility clustering, fat tails and cross-asset correlation preserved — rescaled to forward volatility and re-centred on forward expected returns. Only the shape of the past survives; none of its level does.

The Monte Carlo finds the growth-optimal leverage at 1.25×, entirely independently of the analytic Kelly solution of 1.18× — different machinery, same answer. Note the last column: leverage makes it more likely, not less, that ten years of equity risk leaves you behind a Treasury bill. At 2× that happens in 45% of paths.

Stretch the horizon to 20 years and the ranking is unchanged — median CAGR still peaks at 1.00–1.25× (6.7%) and still falls to 5.2% at 2× — but the forced-sale risk compounds: the chance of at least one margin call at 2× rises from 52% over ten years to 78% over twenty, and at 1.75× from 18% to 33%. A longer horizon does not make high leverage safer; it makes the call arrive.

The US / international split is a knife edge

On the yields alone, international equities have the higher expected return — a 2.90% dividend yield against 1.17%. The optimiser responds by putting everything there. It should not be believed.

That is the shape of the problem: the optimiser has a firm view on how much equity and no real view on which. Hold market weights — roughly 63% US, 37% international — and spend your conviction on the leverage decision, which is the one the numbers can actually support.

The rule, so you can re-run it without me

Everything above reduces to one expression, and every input to it is published daily.

L* = (expected equity return − broker's borrowing rate) / volatility², for the levered part, and (expected return − cash rate) / volatility² below 1×.

Re-run it when any of three things move: the dividend yield (a 20% market decline raises it, and raises the Kelly leverage with it — the rule tells you to buy into falls, which is the opposite of what the drawdown makes you want to do); the borrowing rate, which floats with the fed funds rate and is currently taking a third of your Kelly leverage; and volatility, which enters squared and therefore matters most. A move from 16% to 24% volatility more than halves the growth-optimal leverage on its own.

The revised answer

  • Hold global equities at market weights, roughly 1.0× to 1.25× gross. That is where both the analytic Kelly (1.18×) and the Monte Carlo median (1.25×) land, and neither has any margin-call risk over ten years. In a $50k account that is $50k–$62.5k of VT-equivalent exposure, financed with at most $12.5k of margin at 5.00%.
  • The honest fractional-Kelly answer is lower still. Half Kelly on these inputs is 0.59× — 60% equities, 40% earning 3.80% in cash. Everything on the previous two pages argues for fractioning Kelly rather than taking it whole; the reason not to go that low is that at 1.0× you are already inside the flat part of the growth curve, and the fractional discipline matters most where the curve is steep.
  • No bonds while you are borrowing. Their expected return is below the margin rate. If you want less risk, hold less equity and more cash — not bonds bought with borrowed money.
  • 1.5× is a defensible choice, but it is now a view, not an optimisation. It requires believing the equity premium is about a point higher than the yields imply. It costs 16pp of median drawdown for roughly zero expected median return, and it is still safe from a forced sale — so it is a bet on the upper tail, taken knowingly.
  • 1.75× and above are now indefensible. An 18–52% chance of a margin call over ten years, at a lower median return than 1×.

Where this could be wrong

  • Real growth of 1.5% is the load-bearing assumption, and it is not observable. It is roughly the long-run US real earnings-per-share growth rate; a case for 2.5% (AI-driven margin expansion, say) moves full Kelly to about 1.5×. The sensitivity table above is the honest form of that argument.
  • The valuation term is set to zero, which is a choice in both directions. US equities at a high multiple have historically de-rated over subsequent decades, which would make 6.31% optimistic; assuming any specific reversion, however, is a market-timing forecast in a build-up that is otherwise built from observables.
  • The international premium is a valuation-and-composition artefact. The higher dividend yield partly reflects a different sector mix — more banks and energy, less software — not a free lunch. That is precisely why the knife-edge table matters more than the base-case tilt.
  • Five-year volatility understates a crisis. Forward paths bootstrap real daily shocks, so 2020-style clustering is present, but if forward volatility runs at 20% rather than 16.4% every Kelly number here falls by a third.
  • Yields move. Every number on this page is dated 20 August 2026. That is the point of the rule in the previous section.
