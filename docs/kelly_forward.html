Kelly at Today's Yields

Run on 33 years of history, Kelly asked for 17× gross exposure and a backtested ladder rewarded leverage all the way to the Reg-T cap. Run on what these four asset classes actually yield today, full Kelly is 1.18× equities and nothing in bonds — and the growth-optimal leverage is roughly “fully invested”. The entire difference is the risk premium, and none of it is a modelling choice. Granting the strongest version of the case that modern companies compound faster moves that to 1.50×; the median of 150 years moves it to 0.90×. The whole defensible range is 0.90× to 1.50×, and none of it reaches the Reg-T cap.

The inputs, and where each number comes from

Bonds: the index yield to maturity, which is what a constant-maturity bond fund earns over a holding period as the pull to par offsets price moves. Equities: the Grinold-Kroner build-up — income you can observe, plus growth and inflation you have to assume, stated separately so you can move them one at a time.

Kelly leverage scales with the premium and inversely with variance. Halve the premium and you halve the leverage; there is no other moving part.

Bonds get nothing, and the reason is arithmetic

US bonds yield 4.85% and hedged foreign bonds 4.63%, against cash at 3.80% — a premium of about one point. Robinhood lends at 5.00%.

You cannot borrow at 5.00% to buy a 4.70% expected return. That single inequality removes both bond sleeves from every levered solution: at zero financing cost the optimiser wants 2.4× bonds, and at any realistic retail borrowing spread it wants none. Bonds re-enter only below 1× gross, as a substitute for cash — and at a 3.80% bill rate they are barely that either, since the extra point of yield costs six points of volatility and now moves with equities rather than against them.

One boundary on that claim, developed below: it is a statement about a levered book. Below 1× gross nothing is borrowed, the 5.00% hurdle is replaced by the 3.80% bill rate, and bonds re-enter in size — see the geometric frontier.

This is the cleanest example on any of these pages of a conclusion that is structural rather than statistical. It does not depend on 33 years of data, on which fund proxied which asset class, or on the survivorship problem that made the historical foreign-bond weight untrustworthy. It depends on two numbers you can look up today.

The backtest ladder, re-run forward

400 bootstrapped ten-year paths through the same Reg-T account: historical daily shocks — volatility clustering, fat tails and cross-asset correlation preserved — rescaled to forward volatility and re-centred on forward expected returns. Only the shape of the past survives; none of its level does.

The geometric frontier

Every portfolio on this page is a point on one curve: expected compound growth against volatility. Full Kelly is its maximum. Everything below the maximum is a choice to give up growth for a calmer ride — and how you give it up matters more than most people assume.

Fractional Kelly by slope, not by scaling

The usual way to build a ½-Kelly portfolio is to halve the full-Kelly weights and hold the rest in cash. That is correct in exactly one world: a single interest rate for both borrowing and lending, and no position limits. Two-fund separation then guarantees that every sensible portfolio is the same risky book, diluted.

Neither condition holds in a real account. Borrowing costs 5.00% while cash earns 3.80%, which puts a kink in the problem at 1× gross. So the frontier here is solved by slope instead: at each fraction f the whole portfolio is re-optimised as max w'μ − (1/2f) w'Σw, which is the Kelly objective at f = 1 and a more risk-averse investor below it. Composition is allowed to change, not just size.

What that reveals: the spread sorts the universe

The earlier conclusion — that bonds earn nothing — was a statement about a levered book, and the sharper version is a single test. A sleeve is worth holding with borrowed money only if its premium clears the 1.2pp financing spread. The aggregate bond sleeves earn about a point over cash, so they belong at or below 1× gross and nowhere above it. Long Treasuries (+2.67%) and REITs (+4.70%) clear it comfortably and stay in the book at every leverage level.

Below 1× nothing is borrowed at all, so the hurdle drops to the 3.80% bill rate and the hedged foreign bond sleeve takes up to 30% of capital. The scaling construction can never find that, because it only ever dilutes a portfolio that holds none of it — which is what costs it up to 0.71pp/yr of growth at the same volatility.

US aggregate bonds take zero weight at every point, which is worth flagging rather than celebrating: they are 0.92 correlated with long Treasuries, so the optimiser simply picks the more concentrated form of the same duration. That is a corner solution of the kind a near-singular pair reliably produces.

Three regimes, set by the borrowing spread

  • Below 9.3% volatility: cash and hedged foreign bonds do most of the work, with a small sleeve of everything else. Cash is held only here.
  • 9.3% to 13.9% volatility — exactly 1.00× gross. A band of risk appetites all sit at fully invested, borrowing nothing and holding no cash; what changes across the band is the mix, rotating out of aggregate bonds and into equity and REITs. This flat spot is the borrowing spread's fingerprint: the first dollar of margin costs 1.2pp more than the last dollar of cash earns, so the optimiser sits at the kink rather than crossing it.
  • Above 13.9% volatility: the book borrows, and holds only the four sleeves whose premium beats the spread — the two equity sleeves, long Treasuries and REITs.

Note where half Kelly lands under this construction: 1.00× gross, 32% US equity, 19% international, 28% long Treasuries, 21% REITs — a recognisable balanced portfolio, reached without ever assuming one, and one the scaling construction could not have produced at any fraction.

Where the 1.50% growth assumption comes from

It is the one number in the build-up that is neither observed nor derived, so it deserves more than a footnote. It is also the number every equity conclusion on this page rests on.

What it is, precisely

1.50% is aggregate real earnings growth — not earnings per share. The buyback yield is the separate term that converts one into the other. What an index-fund holder actually earns in growth is the sum of the two: 1.50% + 1.30% = 2.80% per share, per year, in real terms. Getting this backwards is the classic error in this decomposition — drop a historical per-share growth rate into the growth slot while a buyback term sits beside it, and the share count is counted twice.

Run it the other way and the arithmetic gets stark. For US equities to return a round 8%/yr, per-share real growth must be 4.49% — higher than any 30-year stretch since 1871. To repeat the 10.82%/yr they actually delivered from 1993 to 2026 would take 7.31%, roughly double the best ever recorded. Neither is a statement about growth being weak; it is a statement about the starting dividend yield. That yield averaged about 4.4% across the historical record and is 1.17% today, and the missing three points have to come from somewhere.

Why 1.50% and not the 3.3% the record implies

Aggregate real earnings growth since 1871 was roughly 3.3%/yr — the 1.80% per-share figure plus the ~1.5% a year of net new share issuance that diluted it, and close to real GDP growth over the same span. Two things argue against extrapolating that:

  • The economy grows more slowly now. Potential US real GDP growth is generally put nearer 1.8% than the 3.3% of the era that produced the historical figure, on demographics alone. Corporate earnings cannot outgrow the economy indefinitely.
  • The profit share of GDP is at a record high. A large part of the 1985–2023 acceleration to 3.9% was margin expansion — earnings growing faster than output — which is a level shift, not a growth rate. Extrapolating it assumes margins keep rising from a record.

The counter-argument deserves stating: if AI-driven margin expansion is real and durable, 2.5% aggregate growth is defensible, which puts per-share growth at 3.8% — above anything in the record — and full Kelly near 1.5×. That is precisely the belief the 1.5× rung requires, and it is a coherent one. It is just not what the yields say, and it should be held deliberately rather than inherited from a backtest.

“Companies are better at this than they used to be”

The obvious objection to anchoring on 150 years is that the last forty look structurally different, and that recent history is the better guide. That argument is right about something large and real — and the decomposition below separates the part of it that can repeat from the part that cannot.

What the argument gets right — and it is worth about three points a year

Look at the dilution column, not the growth column. From 1947 to 1985, aggregate corporate profits grew at 3.75%/yr in real terms while the shareholder's earnings per share grew at 1.64% — 2.12 percentage points a year leaked away into new share issuance. By 2010–2023 that number is −0.84%: buybacks now add most of a point a year to per-share growth rather than subtracting two.

That is a swing of roughly three percentage points a year in shareholders' favour, and it is exactly the thing the argument is pointing at. It is not a cyclical fluke or a measurement artefact. Buybacks became practical in 1982, capital allocation is genuinely more disciplined than the conglomerate era, and forty years is long enough to call it a regime rather than a phase. Nothing in this analysis assumes it reverses.

What it gets wrong — and it is worth about the same

The other half of 1985–2023's 3.86% is the profit-share column: +1.75%/yr from corporate profits rising from about 5% of GDP to 11.4%. That is not a growth rate, it is a level shift. The profit share doubled; it cannot double again, because 23% of GDP as corporate profit is not a world that exists. Even holding it permanently at today's record — which is what this page's base case does — contributes exactly zero to future growth. The 1985–2023 rate can only be extrapolated by assuming margins keep climbing from an all-time high.

Two smaller pieces of the same era point the same way: the statutory corporate tax rate fell from 46% to 21%, and interest expense fell with a forty-year decline in rates. Both raised the level of after-tax earnings. Neither can happen twice.

One trap worth naming. If you adopt the 3.86% figure, the buyback yield must come out of the build-up — 3.86% is already per share and contains the buyback effect. Adding 1.30% on top gives 5.16% and an equity return near 8.7%, which is not a bolder forecast, it is the share count counted twice.

Where it lands

The honest range across these positions is full Kelly between 0.90× and 1.50×, and it is worth seeing what that does and does not change. It does not resurrect bonds — they still yield less than the margin rate under every scenario. It does not touch the knife-edge US/international split. It does not make 1.75× or 2× safe: the Monte Carlo's margin-call probabilities are driven by volatility, not by expected return, so an 18–52% chance of a forced sale survives every growth assumption on this page.

What it does change is the top of the sensible range. Under the modern-regime assumptions, full Kelly is 1.50× — landing exactly on the rung the historical backtest recommended, by an entirely different route. Two methods with almost nothing in common agreeing on a number is the most reassuring thing on this page.

The rule, so you can re-run it without me

Everything above reduces to one expression, and every input to it is published daily.

L* = (expected equity return − broker's borrowing rate) / volatility², for the levered part, and (expected return − cash rate) / volatility² below 1×.

Worked at today's numbers.

Re-run it when any of three things move: the dividend yield (a 20% market decline raises it, and raises the Kelly leverage with it — the rule tells you to buy into falls, which is the opposite of what the drawdown makes you want to do); the borrowing rate, which floats with the fed funds rate and is currently taking a third of your Kelly leverage; and volatility, which enters squared and therefore matters most. A move from 16% to 24% volatility more than halves the growth-optimal leverage on its own.

The revised answer

  • Hold global equities at market weights, roughly 1.0× to 1.25× gross. That is where both the analytic Kelly (1.18×) and the Monte Carlo median (1.25×) land, and neither has any margin-call risk over ten years. In a $50k account that is $50k–$62.5k of VT-equivalent exposure, financed with at most $12.5k of margin at 5.00%.
  • 1.50× is the ceiling, and it has a specific price of admission: believing the last forty years of per-share earnings growth repeat in full — the end of dilution and nothing given back on margins. The first half of that is well evidenced; the second is an assumption about an all-time-high profit share. Hold it deliberately.
  • The honest fractional-Kelly answer is lower still. Half Kelly on these inputs is 0.59× — 60% equities, 40% earning 3.80% in cash. Everything on the previous two pages argues for fractioning Kelly rather than taking it whole; the reason not to go that low is that at 1.0× you are already inside the flat part of the growth curve, and the fractional discipline matters most where the curve is steep.
  • No bonds while you are borrowing — but bonds, not cash, below 1×. Above 1× gross their expected return is below the margin rate, so they earn nothing. Below it nothing is borrowed and they compete against a 3.80% bill instead, which they beat: the frontier holds up to half the portfolio in bonds at low risk levels, and reaching for less risk by diluting with cash rather than re-optimising costs up to 0.74pp/yr of growth.
  • 1.5× is a defensible choice, but it is now a view, not an optimisation. It requires believing the equity premium is about a point higher than the yields imply. It costs 16pp of median drawdown for roughly zero expected median return, and it is still safe from a forced sale — so it is a bet on the upper tail, taken knowingly.
  • 1.75× and above are now indefensible. An 18–52% chance of a margin call over ten years, at a lower median return than 1×.

Where this could be wrong

  • Real growth of 1.5% is the load-bearing assumption, and it is not observable. It is an aggregate rate, deliberately below the ~3.3%/yr the US corporate sector delivered since 1871, on the grounds that potential GDP growth is lower now and the profit share cannot keep rising from a record. Paired with buybacks it still implies above-median per-share growth, so the base case is not a pessimistic one — the section on it gives the full distribution.
  • The valuation term is set to zero, which is a choice in both directions. US equities at a high multiple have historically de-rated over subsequent decades, which would make 6.31% optimistic; assuming any specific reversion, however, is a market-timing forecast in a build-up that is otherwise built from observables.
  • The international premium is a valuation-and-composition artefact. The higher dividend yield partly reflects a different sector mix — more banks and energy, less software — not a free lunch. That is precisely why the knife-edge table matters more than the base-case tilt.
  • Five-year volatility understates a crisis. Forward paths bootstrap real daily shocks, so 2020-style clustering is present, but if forward volatility runs at 20% rather than 16.4% every Kelly number here falls by a third.
  • Yields move. Every number on this page is dated 20 August 2026. That is the point of the rule in the previous section.
