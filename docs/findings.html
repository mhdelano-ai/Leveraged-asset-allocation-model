Leveraged multi-asset allocation · 1971–2026

Drawdowns not exceeding the S&P 500

The instinct is that this constraint is about surviving 2008. It isn’t. The S&P’s crashes leave an enormous budget that trend-following and a drawdown throttle stay comfortably inside. What actually caps leverage is the other end of the distribution — the years when the index barely fell at all.

The binding constraint is calm markets, not crises

Over 1971–2026 the S&P’s worst three-year drawdown was 55.3%. Its best three-year drawdown was 5.6% — the stretch ending in early 1994. A strategy promising never to be deeper than the index in any three-year window must therefore also stay inside 5.6% during that window, with no crisis in sight.

So the rolling reading is not “don’t crash as hard as the index.” It is be smoother than the index during the index’s smoothest years — a far stronger demand, and one that caps unconditional risk, and therefore return, below the benchmark’s. Which reading you pick changes the answer completely.

Where this gets uncomfortable

The strict solution is fragile, and that is the most important thing on this page.

  • The in-sample optimum clears the constraint by 8 basis points and has a plateau radius of 0% — a 5% move in a single parameter breaks feasibility.
  • sigma_target is the binding axis at every margin level. Feasibility dies the moment the risk target rises, so there is no clever structure buying extra return here: the constraint is a pure cap on how much volatility the book may run.
  • Demanding real slack costs return — 1pp of margin gives 9.10%, 2pp gives 8.16%, 3pp gives 7.28%, and nothing at all survives a 5pp margin.
  • Cross-panel, the strict solution fails. Parameters chosen on the 1971+ panel breach by +0.80pp on the 1986+ nine-sleeve panel — a different universe and a different era.
  • The effective sample size is about seven independent drawdown events, not 13,900 trading days. For a constraint defined on drawdowns, seven is the number that matters.

Box spreads win

By about 97bp a year over leveraged ETFs — the widest gap in the table.

The ETF penalty isn’t mainly cost

No usable 3× commodity, REIT or credit fund exists, so those sleeves are held unlevered and crowd out leverage elsewhere. The ETF book is structurally less diversified.

No calls, ever

At constraint-feasible leverage (~1.1×) forced liquidation never triggers, so the ETF wrapper’s one real advantage is worth nothing while its cost is real.

On “LETF drag”: UPRO’s gross gap to 3× the index is ~5.2% a year, but 2.90pp of that is simply the cost of borrowing two units of cash, which any vehicle pays. The wrapper-specific cost is 2.27% a year — about 2.5× the stated 0.91% expense ratio.

How it is built

Allocation

  • Inverse-volatility weights with cluster risk budgets
  • Time-series momentum, measured in excess of cash
  • Volatility targeting, floored by a crisis-correlation covariance
  • CPPI-style drawdown throttle with a dead zone and hysteresis

Why both trend and vol targeting

Volatility targeting lags by construction — realised vol only rises after prices fall, so it stays invested through slow bears like 2000–02 and 2022. Trend exits those but whipsaws in fast crashes, where the vol response is the effective brake. They fail in different places.

Data

S&P total return rebuilt from price plus Shiller dividends (−4.7bp/yr against the actual index); Treasury returns from constant-maturity par-bond rolls (+1bp/yr against TLT); LBMA gold daily to 1968. Every synthetic series validated against a traded instrument before use.

Traps worth knowing about

Each of these fails silently and yields a plausible-looking backtest.

  • pandas 3.0 stores datetimes at microsecond resolution. The familiar np.diff(index.asi8) / 86_400e9 idiom then produces day counts 1000× too small, zeroing out every time-scaled accrual at once — dividends, coupons and the cost of leverage — while leaving price returns untouched. It cost the S&P reconstruction 230bp a year.
  • Yahoo returns quarterly bars for range=max&interval=1d — about 168 points instead of 8,400, with nothing in the response saying so.
  • Yahoo also returns valid-but-truncated payloads. Two Treasury yield series cached with 15 rows instead of 16,141 and parsed perfectly.
  • ^GSPC’s adjusted close equals its close — for indices it carries no dividends at all.
  • Shiller’s price column is a monthly average. October 1987 reads ~280 against an intramonth low of 224.84, so using it for the price path erases Black Monday entirely.
  • FRED licence-truncates some series while returning well-formed CSV — and it throttled hard enough to be unreachable for this entire build, so Treasury yields come from Yahoo’s index equivalents instead.
