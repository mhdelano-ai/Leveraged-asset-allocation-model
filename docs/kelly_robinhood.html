The Robinhood Leverage Ladder

Kelly says the allocation is US equities and the decision is how much of it to own. In a margin account that decision is not made by the growth-optimal calculation — it is made by the maintenance requirement. 1.5× is the highest leverage that was never force-sold in 33 years; above it, the extra return exists only for someone who re-levers the day after being liquidated.

The ladder

100% US equities, rebalanced monthly to a fixed gross exposure, simulated daily through a Reg-T account: 25% maintenance, requirements hiked as the market falls, tested against intraday prices, borrowing at Robinhood's 5.00% against a 3.80% bill — a 1.2pp spread.

The number that actually decides it

A margin call is not a risk-management event, it is a mechanical one: it fires at a fixed distance below your last rebalance, and you can compute that distance before you place the trade.

How far the position can fall between monthly rebalances before the broker sells.
Gross exposureTextbook 25% ruleWith intraday testing and a procyclical hikeComparable event
1.25×−73.3%−63.9%worse than 2008 peak-to-trough

2.00×−33.3%−20.4%2020, 2022, 2002 — all of them

Speed matters more than depth. The distance is measured from the last rebalance, not from the peak — and rebalancing monthly to a fixed leverage sells on the way down. That is why 1.5× came through 2008's slow 55% decline untouched while 2× was called six times, and why the crash that breaks a levered account is a fast one, not necessarily a deep one.

Above 1.5×, the return is a discipline test

A forced sale only costs you the recovery if you do not put the position back on. The simulation below is identical except in what happens after the call: the investor either re-levers at the next rebalance, or stays where the broker left them.

At 2×, flinching once costs more than the entire gain from leverage: the account ends at 11.26%/yr against 10.86% for never having borrowed at all — 40bp/yr, for a −77% drawdown. The whole case for going above 1.5× rests on buying back in, with borrowed money, in October 2008. Assume you will not, and the ladder stops at 1.5×.

Does the safe rung move if the broker is harsher?

Robinhood publishes a 25%–100% maintenance range and sets it per security from its own volatility model, so 25% is a floor, not a promise. This is that assumption stressed.

What to hold in it

The allocation question is nearly settled by the previous page: at these leverage levels Kelly wants equities, and international equities and bonds earn nothing. What is worth pricing is how much that concentration is costing you if you would rather not make it.

Adding 20% international equity cost 0.56pp/yr of log growth and bought no drawdown protection — the 1.5× drawdown is marginally worse, because international equities fell just as hard while returning less. That is a real historical fact and a weak basis for a forward-looking decision: it is 33 years of one country outperforming, and the same table run in 2008 would have said the opposite. Treat a 10–20% international sleeve as insurance you are knowingly paying about half a point a year for, not as an error.

Bonds are different. They are not diversification here — at 1.5× gross they simply crowd out the asset doing the work, which is why 90/10 earns less than 100/0 with an almost identical drawdown. If you want less risk, take the leverage down; do not add bonds and then borrow against them.

A 2× daily-reset fund held for 33 years returned less than the unlevered index at nearly twice the drawdown. The daily reset compounds 2 × r, so volatility drag scales with the square of the multiplier, and the fund's all-in financing is wider than a margin loan. Its one genuine advantage — it cannot be margin-called, and the worst case is losing what you put in — is the reason to use one tactically, not the reason to hold one.

Mechanics, specific to Robinhood

  • The rate. 5.00% on margin balances up to $50k as of July 2026, stepping down by tier; Gold ($5/month) makes the first $1,000 of margin interest-free, which roughly pays for itself. Against a 3.80% bill that is a 1.2pp spread — genuinely cheap, and the single biggest reason this ladder is worth climbing at all. It floats with the fed funds rate; at bills + 2.5% the 1.5× rung loses 0.7pp/yr.
  • The requirement is theirs, not the regulator's. 25% is FINRA's floor; Robinhood's published range is 25%–100% and is set per security by their own model, including concentration. A one-position VTI account is the friendliest possible case, and it is still their call, changeable overnight. Size for 30%.
  • No portfolio margin. Reg-T caps you at 2× at purchase regardless. The cheap-financing tricks in the rest of this project — box spreads at bills + 20bp, 3× portfolio margin — are not available here.
  • Rebalance monthly, mechanically. The simulation's zero-call record at 1.5× depends on it: a fixed-leverage rebalance sells into declines. Drifting up to 1.9× in a rally and meeting the next crash there is how a 1.5× plan becomes a 2× outcome.
  • Taxes are not modelled and are not small. In a taxable account monthly rebalancing realises short-term gains; margin interest is deductible only against investment income and only if you itemise. The 13.60% at 1.5× is pre-tax.
  • Keep the borrowing away from the emergency fund. A margin call is met by depositing cash or by the broker selling; the whole ladder assumes the money in the account is money you will not need during a −70% stretch lasting years.

What this analysis does not know

  • “100% US equity” is the answer the last 33 years gave. It is a single-country, single-asset bet chosen by an optimiser looking at the period in which that country won. The bootstrap on the previous page could not distinguish these risk premia at all; the leverage conclusion is far more robust than the allocation one.
  • Every CAGR here is in-sample. Held forward with weights re-estimated in real time, the same machinery returned 9.99%/yr against 11.53% for owning US equities and never optimising. The ladder's ranking of leverage levels is the durable finding; the levels of the returns are not.
  • 33 years contains about three real tests — 2000–02, 2007–09, 2020. A leverage rule validated on three events is a rule with a sample size of three.
  • The 2013–2026 window flatters everything. On the modern all-index panel the same ladder shows 14.0% at 1× and 18.9% at 1.5× with a −50% drawdown, and even 2× was only called in March 2020. That period is not a fair test and those numbers should not be planned around.
