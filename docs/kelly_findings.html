Kelly-optimal allocation · four asset classes · 1993–2026

Ruin at Full Kelly

The log-optimal portfolio of US equities, international ex-US equities, US bonds and international ex-US bonds, computed from history exactly as asked — and then held. Full Kelly wants 17× gross exposure, and an account that followed it using only data available at the time was wiped out in September 2008. The usable answers are all fractions of it.

The two answers

Both are Kelly. They differ only in whether the portfolio is allowed to borrow — and that single switch changes the allocation beyond recognition.

The uncomfortable part: once leverage is taken away, log-optimal is all equities and nothing else. Kelly is not an argument for diversification at 1×; it is an argument for leverage.

Why bonds only appear when borrowing is allowed

Kelly maximises expected log wealth. At 1× exposure a 15%-volatility equity book gives up only about 1.1pp/yr to variance drag — nowhere near enough to make a 4%-return bond sleeve worth its place. Bonds earn a Kelly weight only when the optimiser can borrow to lever their Sharpe ratio up, which is exactly what full Kelly does, and exactly what ruins it.

The Kelly fraction, in sample against out of sample

In sample, growth rises all the way to f = 1 — that is what “optimal” means, and it is not evidence of anything. Out of sample, with weights re-estimated every year from data available at the time, growth peaks near half Kelly and then falls off a cliff.

The answer, stated plainly

  • Full Kelly on these four asset classes is not a portfolio you can hold. It asks for 17× gross exposure, and its composition is dominated by whichever low-volatility sleeve happened to post the best Sharpe ratio in the sample.
  • Fitted in real time it goes bankrupt — September 2008, on a −113.9% month. That is not a quirk of one path: everything from f = 0.90 upward ruins.
  • Out of sample, growth peaks around half Kelly at 15.0%/yr — at a −88.9% drawdown, which is a growth optimum almost nobody's actual risk tolerance would accept. Quarter Kelly, 11.2%/yr at −60%, is the first row on the table that resembles an investable strategy.
  • Without leverage, the Kelly portfolio is 100% US equities. In both panels, in the bootstrap median, and with shrunk means. International equities never earn a weight; bonds earn one only when they can be levered.
  • The weights are not identified by the data. Bootstrap intervals span the sign for two of four assets, and each decade produces a completely different portfolio. The honest reading is that 33 years of monthly data cannot distinguish these four risk premia finely enough to support a Kelly allocation at all.

If a number is wanted anyway: quarter Kelly on the leverage-capped, long-only solution — roughly 0.5× US equities against cash, growing to 2× only for an investor who has explicitly accepted a 70%+ drawdown — is the most defensible point on the curve, and it is defensible mainly because it is close to what you would have chosen without an optimiser.

Holding this in an actual account

Everything above is the abstract question. The companion analysis puts the same allocation inside a Reg-T retail margin account — 2× cap, 25% maintenance, the broker's borrowing rate, forced sales simulated daily — and finds that the maintenance requirement, not the growth optimum, sets the leverage: 1.5× was never force-sold in 33 years; 1.75× and 2× were, and their extra return exists only for an investor who re-borrows at the bottom.
