The consolidated answer · four asset classes, one retail account

Where Kelly Lands

Five studies, one conclusion. Own global equities at market weights alongside long Treasuries and REITs, hold aggregate bonds instead of cash at low risk levels, and stop levering at about 1.6×. Everything below is why, and what would have to change for the answer to move.

On a $50,000 account, half Kelly is $16,000 US equity, $9,500 international, $14,000 long Treasuries, $10,500 REITs, no margin. Full Kelly is the same shape levered 1.58×, with $29,000 borrowed at 5.00%. The last row is what over-levering looks like: half again the volatility for less growth than the row above it.

Two sleeves joined the universe after a screen of twelve candidates: long Treasuries and REITs, the only two with an observable yield that earned a weight over both 5- and 10-year risk windows. Gold, commodities, high yield and emerging-market debt did not — gold and commodities because they have no yield to clear the 3.80% cash hurdle, high yield because its spread barely covers expected defaults and it behaves like equity anyway. Adding the two lifts peak growth from 6.86% to 7.28% and moves the growth-optimal leverage from 1.18× to 1.58×.

The curve behind the table

Expected compound growth against volatility. The peak is full Kelly. Everything to the left is buying calm with growth; everything to the right is paying growth for volatility and getting nothing.

The lower band is the part worth studying. Aggregate bonds and cash occupy most of the portfolio at low risk levels and vanish entirely once the book is levered, while long Treasuries and REITs stay in all the way up. That split is not a judgement call: it is one number, the borrowing spread — see the next section.

Three facts do all the work

1. One number sorts the whole universe: the borrowing spread

Cash pays 3.80%; margin costs 5.00%. Below 1× gross nothing is borrowed, so every sleeve competes against the bill rate. Above it, a sleeve must clear the spread to be worth holding with borrowed money. That single test explains the entire shape of the composition band:

The aggregate bond sleeves earn about a point over cash — less than the 1.2pp spread — so they are worth owning with your own money and never with the broker's. Long Treasuries and REITs clear the spread comfortably and stay in the book at every leverage level. Gold clears neither hurdle: with no cash flow, a yield-anchored expected return is inflation and nothing more, and it takes zero weight everywhere.

Reaching for lower risk by diluting the levered book with cash, rather than re-optimising into what the frontier actually wants, costs up to 0.71pp/yr of growth for no reduction in risk. And one honest caveat on long Treasuries: their geometric return of 5.23% only just clears the 5.00% margin rate. The Kelly maths uses arithmetic returns, where a 15.7%-volatility bond picks up 1.24pp from the variance term — correct for maximising log growth, but not a reason to think of them as cheap leverage.

2. Leverage stops at 1.2× because the premium is 4.4pp, not 8.9pp

Kelly leverage is the risk premium divided by variance. Global equities are priced today to return about 6.7% against 3.8% cash — a 4.4-point premium at 16.4% volatility, which alone supports 1.18× once the broker's spread is paid. Adding two more sleeves that clear the spread lowers the volatility per unit of premium and moves the optimum to 1.58×. Backtests recommend far more only because 1993–2026 delivered an 8.9-point premium, mostly from a starting dividend yield of 4.4% against 1.17% today.

3. The broker still decides above 1.75×

A margin call turns a drawdown into a permanent loss: the position is sold at the bottom and is not there for the recovery. Over 400 simulated ten-year paths of the half-Kelly holding, the probability of at least one forced sale is 0% up to 1.75× and 41% at 2×. Those numbers are driven by volatility, not by expected return, so no growth assumption makes them go away — but a better-diversified book does push the danger further out. The same simulation on pure equity called at 1.75× in 18% of paths and at 2× in 52%.

The 1993–2026 backtest agrees from the other direction: run through a Reg-T account, the half-Kelly holding was never force-sold at 1.5×, returning 10.86%/yr at 18.6% volatility — the same return as 100% US equity unlevered, which is what a diversified book looks like in an era one country won. At 1× it returned 8.79% at 12.2% volatility and a −45.6% worst drawdown, against 10.86% at 18.3% and −55.3% for the US equity book.

If money is going in or coming out

Kelly's fraction is derived for a closed account. The problem is scale-invariant there, so the optimum does not depend on how much is in it. Almost nobody is in that situation — and it turns out the direction of the flow matters far less than whether the flow is a fixed size or a fixed percentage.

Proportional flows change nothing at all

Drawing 4% of the current balance keeps the problem scale-invariant: the withdrawal shrinks with the account, so it can go on forever without ever emptying it. The optimum stays where the closed-account answer put it, and ruin is impossible at every leverage level.

Look at the two 5th-percentile columns. A proportional drawdown leaves a bad path with 22–44 cents on the dollar. A fixed one leaves it with nothing, at every leverage level tested. The same 4%, sized two different ways, is the difference between a portfolio that shrinks and a portfolio that dies.

So median wealth is the wrong thing to maximise once you are drawing a fixed income from the account. It still points at 1.50× — and 1.50× carries a one-in-five chance of running out of money over thirty years. If the objective is instead “keep the ruin probability under 5%”, the answer is 0.5× or less, and the whole leverage question inverts.

Leverage cannot rescue a withdrawal rate that is too high

At 6% of the opening balance against a portfolio expected to return 6.9%, more than half of all paths run out of money at every leverage level. Ruin probability is U-shaped — 82% at 0.5×, a minimum of 51% around 1.25–1.5×, back to 56% at 2× — because you are ground down by withdrawals at the low end and killed by volatility at the high end. There is no leverage that fixes it. The withdrawal rate is the problem.

Deposits barely move the optimum, and transform the downside

Paying in 20%/yr of the opening balance over twenty years leaves the growth-optimal leverage where it was — 1.75× with flows and without. What changes is everything below the median: at 2× gross the 5th-percentile outcome goes from 0.44× your starting money to 3.01×, because the contributions come to dominate the balance. Deposits do not make leverage a better bet. They make the outcome depend far less on it.

One mechanical trap worth knowing. In these simulations the margin-call probability is identical to the decimal whether you are saving or not — 1.2% at 1.75×, 63% at 2×. That is not a bug: rebalancing monthly to a fixed leverage ratio resets the maintenance ratio regardless of how big the account has become, so new money buys no safety. To make contributions act as a margin buffer you have to target a constant debt instead — borrow a fixed dollar amount and let leverage drift down as you pay in.

The lifecycle bound: your future deposits are a bond you already own

If the Kelly fraction is the right share of total economic wealth to hold in risk assets, and total wealth is the account plus the present value of what you will still pay in, then the leverage to run on the account alone is L × (1 + PV / account):

Reg-T caps you at 2×. For a young saver the binding constraint is the broker, not Kelly — which is the useful finding, because it means the leverage decision early in a savings life is “as much as they will let me, within the margin-call limits” rather than a fine calculation. The assumption underneath is that a salary is a Treasury bond, which it is not: job risk correlates with markets exactly when it hurts most, and no broker will lend against it. Treat the number as an upper bound, never a target.

The short version

  • Saving into the account: keep the leverage from the closed-account answer — 1.0× to 1.5× — and know that the contributions, not the leverage, are doing most of the work. Theory says go higher; the broker and the honesty of the human-capital assumption both say don't.
  • Drawing a fixed income: the closed-account answer no longer applies. Ruin is now possible, median wealth is the wrong objective, and 0.5× or unlevered is where a 5%-ruin budget lands. Leverage in decumulation buys upside you do not need with money you cannot replace.
  • Drawing a percentage: Kelly is untouched. If you can make your spending flex with the portfolio, you get the closed-account answer back and ruin off the table entirely — which is a strong argument for a percentage-of-balance drawdown rule quite apart from anything to do with leverage.

What would move the answer

One assumption carries the equity conclusion, and it is not observable: how fast real earnings per share grow. Everything else in the model is a published number.

The case that modern companies compound faster is half right, and the half that is right is already in the base case. Dilution — the leakage from new share issuance — ran at 2.12%/yr against shareholders from 1947 to 1985 and is negative today: buybacks now add about 0.84pp/yr. That is a genuine forty-year regime change, granted here in full. The other half of the modern era's growth was corporate profits rising from 5% to 11.4% of GDP, which is a level shift: the share doubled and cannot double again.

The rule, so you can re-run it when yields move: L* = (expected equity return − borrowing rate) / volatility². Re-check it when the dividend yield changes (a market fall raises Kelly leverage), when your margin rate changes, or when volatility changes — it enters squared, so 16% to 24% more than halves the answer.

What I am least confident about

  • The US / international split is a knife edge. A 2pp swing in an unobservable growth assumption moves it from all-international to all-US, while total equity exposure barely moves. That is why it is set at market weights rather than optimised — the numbers support a view on how much equity, not on which.
  • Every expected return here is a model, not a forecast. The bond numbers are close to mechanical; the equity number rests on a growth assumption that has been wrong by two points in either direction over thirty-year stretches.
  • Volatility is measured over five years. If forward volatility runs at 20% rather than 16.4%, every leverage number here falls by roughly a third.
  • Taxes are not modelled. In a taxable account, rebalancing realises short-term gains and margin interest is deductible only against investment income, and only if you itemise. All returns quoted are pre-tax.
  • The record is thin where it matters. Thirty-three years of backtest contains about three real tests of a levered book — 2000–02, 2007–09, 2020.
  • Six sleeves is a harder estimation problem than four. Resampling the risk estimate leaves the half-Kelly weights stable (long Treasuries 0.23–0.33, REITs 0.16–0.26 across 300 block-bootstrap draws) — but that test holds the expected returns fixed, and the expected returns are the fragile part. Every sleeve added is another mean that cannot really be estimated.
  • US aggregate bonds now take zero weight, which is a strong claim. Long Treasuries are 0.92 correlated with them and yield more per unit of duration, so the optimiser simply prefers the concentrated form. That is a real result, and also exactly the sort of corner solution a near-singular pair produces. Holding some of the duration as BND rather than all of it as TLT costs very little and is easier to live with.
  • The REIT weight rests on an assumption. Its 6.71% expected return is a 3.87% distribution yield plus inflation plus 0.5% of assumed real growth. REITs are also 0.67 correlated with equities, so much of what looks like diversification is equity beta wearing a different label.
