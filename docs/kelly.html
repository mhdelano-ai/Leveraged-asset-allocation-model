Where Kelly Lands

Three studies, one conclusion. Own global equities at market weights, add bonds instead of cash whenever you hold less than 100% equity, and stop levering at about 1.2×. Everything below is why, and what would have to change for the answer to move.

The curve behind the table

Expected compound growth against volatility. The peak is full Kelly. Everything to the left is buying calm with growth; everything to the right is paying growth for volatility and getting nothing.

The lower band is the part worth studying: bonds occupy up to half the portfolio at low risk levels and vanish entirely above 16.4% volatility. That is not a judgement call, it is the borrowing spread — see the next section.

Three facts do all the work

1. Bonds are worth holding, until you borrow

US bonds yield 4.85% and hedged foreign bonds 4.63%. Cash pays 3.80%. Margin costs 5.00%. So bonds beat cash and lose to the margin rate — which means they belong in every portfolio held at or below 1× gross, and in none held above it. Reaching for lower risk by diluting an equity book with cash instead of re-optimising into bonds costs up to 0.73pp/yr of growth for no reduction in risk.

The same spread explains the flat stretch in the middle of the curve: between 6.7% and 16.4% volatility every solution sits at exactly 1.00× gross, rotating out of bonds into equity rather than changing exposure. The first dollar of margin costs 1.2pp more than the last dollar of cash earns, so the optimiser parks at the boundary rather than crossing it.

2. Leverage stops at 1.2× because the premium is 4.4pp, not 8.9pp

Kelly leverage is the risk premium divided by variance. Global equities are priced today to return about 6.7% against 3.8% cash — a 4.4-point premium at 16.4% volatility. That arithmetic gives 1.63× if you could borrow at the bill rate, and 1.18× once the broker's spread is paid. Backtests recommend far more only because 1993–2026 delivered an 8.9-point premium, mostly from a starting dividend yield of 4.4% against 1.17% today.

3. Above 1.5×, the broker decides, not you

A margin call turns a drawdown into a permanent loss: the position is sold at the bottom and is not there for the recovery. Over 400 simulated ten-year paths, the probability of at least one forced sale is 0% at 1.5×, 18% at 1.75×, and 52% at 2× — and over twenty years, 78% at 2×. Those numbers are driven by volatility, not by expected return, so no growth assumption makes them go away.

The 1993–2026 backtest agrees from the other direction: 1.5× was never force-sold in 33 years, 1.75× was called in October 2008, and 2× six times from July 2002. And an investor who declines to re-borrow after being sold out ends up at 11.3%/yr against 10.9% for never having borrowed at all — 40bp, for a 77% drawdown.

What would move the answer

One assumption carries the equity conclusion, and it is not observable: how fast real earnings per share grow. Everything else in the model is a published number.

The case that modern companies compound faster is half right, and the half that is right is already in the base case. Dilution — the leakage from new share issuance — ran at 2.12%/yr against shareholders from 1947 to 1985 and is negative today: buybacks now add about 0.84pp/yr. That is a genuine forty-year regime change, granted here in full. The other half of the modern era's growth was corporate profits rising from 5% to 11.4% of GDP, which is a level shift: the share doubled and cannot double again.

The rule, so you can re-run it when yields move: L* = (expected equity return − borrowing rate) / volatility². Re-check it when the dividend yield changes (a market fall raises Kelly leverage), when your margin rate changes, or when volatility changes — it enters squared, so 16% to 24% more than halves the answer.

What I am least confident about

  • The US / international split is a knife edge. A 2pp swing in an unobservable growth assumption moves it from all-international to all-US, while total equity exposure barely moves. That is why it is set at market weights rather than optimised — the numbers support a view on how much equity, not on which.
  • Every expected return here is a model, not a forecast. The bond numbers are close to mechanical; the equity number rests on a growth assumption that has been wrong by two points in either direction over thirty-year stretches.
  • Volatility is measured over five years. If forward volatility runs at 20% rather than 16.4%, every leverage number here falls by roughly a third.
  • Taxes are not modelled. In a taxable account, rebalancing realises short-term gains and margin interest is deductible only against investment income, and only if you itemise. All returns quoted are pre-tax.
  • The record is thin where it matters. Thirty-three years of backtest contains about three real tests of a levered book — 2000–02, 2007–09, 2020.
