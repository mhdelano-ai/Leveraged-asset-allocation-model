Method and limits
How each number is made
  • Covariance. Nine families of volatility models are averaged within each family and then across families, dropping the highest and lowest for each asset; 25 correlation forecasts are averaged; the result is scaled up to the 12-month horizon, because 12-month returns are more dispersed than short-horizon volatility implies. Short-horizon volatility is measured from 21-day returns, not daily ones.
  • Expected return. Twelve times each asset's average monthly log excess return over its whole history. Its interval allows for serial correlation and pays for the noise in that correction; it covers sampling error only, not an average that has shifted.
  • Predictive range. Centred on the expected return; scaled by the forecast 12-month volatility widened by the expected return's own uncertainty; shaped by past outcomes measured in the same units, recentred, with the 1%, 5%, 95% and 99% points no thinner than a normal of the same spread.
  • No position sizing. The package publishes the inputs; how much leverage to take is the allocation model's decision. For an optimized or leveraged portfolio, the covariance side's own evidence says to scale predicted variance up (expectation 4 above, and the report) or constrain the optimizer.
What the numbers cannot tell you
  • When a crisis starts. The volatility forecast reacts after the fact; the predictive range assumes next year's shape resembles the past's.
  • Whether expected returns have shifted. A historical average is slow by construction, and its interval says nothing about a change of regime.
  • Much about the portfolio tails. The portfolio shape's 1% point rests on a handful of historical episodes, and its spread is narrower than the covariance forecast (first section); the asset-shape row shows how much a different, also defensible, choice moves it.
  • The return side uses the history from — without the month-average price eras; the covariance side uses everything back to the 1870s. US small caps have no carry or valuation measure among the return predictors, and several daily-volatility predictors change level where their data source changes.
  • The backtests can detect only large differences: twelve held-out years can show that nothing collapsed, not which close model is best.
Terms used on this page
Excess return
Log return of the asset minus that of one-month T-bills, summed over the 12 months after the forecast date.
Out-of-sample R²
1 − (model's squared forecast errors) / (historical mean's). Positive means the model's point forecasts were closer.
Detectable R²
Roughly the R² a model would need for 5% significance given the number of non-overlapping years. Below it, a true edge could not be told from luck.
Family test (SPA)
Is the best of a set of models better than the benchmark, allowing for having searched over the set? The p-value to read for "did anything beat it".
CRPS
The error of a whole predictive distribution. A gain is 1 − CRPS(model) / CRPS(benchmark).
Coverage
How often the outcome fell inside a central interval; a calibrated 90% interval holds about 90%.
Calibration test
Whether outcomes fall at the predicted 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles as often as they should; a small p-value means miscalibration.
CER
Certainty-equivalent return: the safe return an investor with risk aversion 5 would accept instead of the strategy.
QLIKE
The volatility loss used throughout the covariance side; lower is better.
Holdout
Years held back and scored once, after the models were fixed.
Independent years
Overlapping 12-month returns share 11 of 12 months, so a decade of monthly forecasts holds about ten independent outcomes.
